The law on phones at the wheel changed in March 2022, and the change was larger than the coverage suggested. It stopped being an offence about *communicating* and became an offence about *holding*.

That single shift closed a loophole drivers had been using successfully in court, and it made a long list of ordinary habits unambiguously illegal.

## What the offence now is

Before 25 March 2022, the wording turned on interactive communication. A driver caught filming out of the windscreen, or scrolling through photos already on the device, could argue — sometimes successfully — that no communication was taking place.

Since that date the offence covers **holding and using a device for almost any purpose**. In practice:

- Taking a photo or video
- Scrolling a playlist or changing a track
- Checking a notification
- Unlocking the screen
- Playing a game
- Entering or adjusting a satnav destination
- Reading anything at all

If you pick it up and touch it, that is the offence. The content on the screen is no longer the question.

## The penalty, and the cliff edge for new drivers

The fixed penalty is **£200 and six penalty points**. Taken to court instead, the fine can reach **£1,000** for a car driver, with disqualification available.

There is **no awareness course** alternative. Lower-end speeding offences frequently come with the option of a course instead of points; this does not.

Six points is where the real severity sits:

- For an established driver, it is **half the twelve** that triggers a totting-up ban. Two offences inside three years is a disqualification.
- For a driver **within two years of passing**, six points **revokes the licence outright**. Back to a provisional, both tests again.

That last outcome deserves emphasis because it is so disproportionate to the moment that causes it. A new driver who glances at a message once, and is seen doing it, loses the entitlement entirely. One offence. Our guide to [speeding fines and the points system](/blog/speeding-fines-bands-points-courses) covers how the thresholds work across offences.

## Stationary does not mean parked

The most common objection — "I was stopped" — does not work.

The offence applies while **driving**, and a vehicle halted at traffic lights, queuing in traffic, or waiting at a junction is still being driven in law. The engine being off under a stop-start system changes nothing.

The same applies when **supervising a learner**. The supervising driver is treated as driving for these purposes, and is as liable as if they held the wheel.

The practical test: if you are in the driving seat and the car is in use on a road, the phone stays down. Being genuinely parked — engine off, safely stopped, not in traffic — is the only state in which picking it up is unremarkable.

## What hands-free still allows

Hands-free remains legal, with one condition that does all the work: **the device is never held.**

Permitted:

- Bluetooth headsets and car systems
- Voice assistants
- Integrated dashboard systems
- A phone in a cradle you do not touch

The important caveat is that legal is not the same as safe, and the law has a second route. If hands-free use causes your standard of driving to drop, **driving without due care and attention** remains available to the police as a charge, carrying three to nine points and an unlimited fine. A collision while deep in a hands-free call is not protected by the fact that the call itself was lawful.

Cradle use sits in a similar grey area. Holding is the offence, so a mounted phone is not it — but a device positioned where it obstructs your view, or operated in a way that visibly distracts, can support a careless driving charge. Setting the destination before moving off is the only version of this that carries no risk at all.

## How it is enforced

Phone offences are harder to automate than speed or tax, so detection remains substantially human — marked and unmarked patrols, motorway camera vans, and increasingly cameras mounted high enough to see into a cab.

Some forces now operate camera systems designed to detect phone use and seat belt non-compliance directly, reviewing flagged images before a notice is issued. Where those are in use, the assumption that nobody can see into the car no longer holds.

A phone offence arrives the same way a speeding one does: a **Notice of Intended Prosecution** to the registered keeper, normally within 14 days, together with a demand to identify the driver. As with speeding, **failing to identify the driver is a separate and worse offence** — six points and a fine of up to £1,000 in its own right.

## The practical habits

The offence is unusual in being almost entirely avoidable with a few mechanical decisions made before you move:

- Put the phone somewhere you **cannot reach** — the glovebox, a bag on the back seat
- Set the route **before moving off**, not at the first red light
- Turn on **Do Not Disturb While Driving**, which most phones will trigger automatically
- Agree with whoever might call that you will not answer, so the instinct to reach for it stops being reflexive
- If a call genuinely matters, **stop properly** — not at lights, not on the hard shoulder except in an emergency

The economics are stark. A phone offence costs £200, six points, several years of elevated insurance premiums, and for a new driver the licence itself. Against that, almost nothing on the screen was worth it.

## The short version

- Since **25 March 2022** it is **holding and using** the device that is the offence, whatever it is for
- **£200 and six points**, or up to **£1,000** and disqualification in court
- **No awareness course** option exists
- **Six points revokes a new driver's licence** — one offence is enough
- **Stationary in traffic is still driving**; so is supervising a learner
- **Hands-free is legal** only while the phone is never held — and careless driving still applies
- Failing to name the driver is a **separate six-point offence**
