Of all the ways to be caught out by motoring law, driving uninsured is the one that most often happens by accident — a cancelled direct debit, a policy that lapsed while the car sat on a driveway, a borrowed car and an assumption about cover that stopped being true years ago.

The law does not grade on intent. Using a vehicle without insurance is a strict liability offence, which means believing you were covered is, by itself, not a defence.

## What it costs

At the roadside, the fixed penalty is **£300 and six penalty points**.

Six points is not an ordinary outcome. For a driver within two years of passing their test, six points **revokes the licence entirely** — back to a provisional, both tests to retake. For everyone else, it is half of the twelve that triggers a ban.

If the case goes to court instead of being dealt with by fixed penalty, the fine is **unlimited** and the court can **disqualify** you outright.

And separately from any of that, police can **seize the vehicle** on the spot. Getting it back requires producing valid insurance and paying recovery and storage fees, which accrue daily. Cars that are not claimed are disposed of — sold or destroyed.

## The IN10, and why insurers care so much

A conviction or fixed penalty for uninsured use is recorded as an **IN10** endorsement, which stays on the driving record for **four years** from the offence date. Insurers ask about convictions for longer than they remain on the licence, so the practical shadow is longer still.

IN10 is among the most expensive endorsements to carry — frequently worse for a premium than a speeding conviction, and sometimes worse than a careless driving charge. The reason is what it signals. Speeding tells an insurer how you drive. Uninsured use tells them whether you buy insurance, and that is a more direct prediction of whether they will be paid.

Some insurers decline the risk altogether, which narrows the market at exactly the point you most need it.

## The offence that happens while the car is parked

This is the part that catches blameless people, because no driving is involved.

Under **Continuous Insurance Enforcement**, a vehicle registered in the UK must be insured at all times — unless it has been declared off the road with a SORN. Not insured while driven. Insured, full stop.

The DVLA runs its record against the **Motor Insurance Database** and identifies vehicles that appear uninsured. The sequence then runs:

1. An **Insurance Advisory Letter** to the registered keeper
2. If ignored, a **£100 fixed penalty**
3. **Clamping or impounding** of the vehicle
4. Court proceedings, with fines up to **£1,000**

Nobody has to drive anywhere for any of that to happen.

The clean way out, if the car genuinely is off the road, is a SORN — free, immediate, and it lifts the insurance requirement entirely. Our guide to [SORN rules and penalties](/blog/sorn-rules-and-penalties) covers where the lines are, including the important detail that a residential street is a public road for this purpose.

## How it is detected

ANPR cameras read plates continuously and check them against the Motor Insurance Database in real time. A vehicle with no insurance record is flagged automatically.

There is no discretion involved and no need to have done anything else wrong first. The older model — where uninsured driving was discovered because someone was stopped for a different reason — has largely gone. Our guide to [how ANPR cameras catch drivers](/blog/anpr-cameras-catching-drivers-2026) covers what the network checks on every read.

One consequence worth drawing out: the database is checked, not the paperwork in your glovebox. If your insurer has not recorded the policy correctly, or a change of vehicle has not propagated, the camera sees an uninsured car. That is resolvable, but it is resolved after the stop, not before it.

## The gaps people fall into

**Driving Other Cars.** Comprehensive policies once routinely included third-party cover to drive someone else's car. That inclusion has been widely withdrawn, and where it survives it is typically third-party only, restricted to policyholders over 25, and void if the other vehicle is not itself insured. Check the certificate rather than relying on what was true the last time you looked.

**A lapsed direct debit.** Insurers cancel for non-payment, and the notification goes to whatever email address they hold. A cancelled policy produces no visible change to the car.

**Cancellation for non-disclosure.** A policy voided after the fact is treated as never having existed, which can make past journeys retrospectively uninsured.

**Named driver confusion.** Being a named driver on a policy covers you on that vehicle. It does not create any entitlement elsewhere.

**Selling the car without telling the DVLA.** If the V5C was never updated, you remain the registered keeper — so Continuous Insurance Enforcement letters, and the penalties behind them, still arrive with your name on them. ## If a letter arrives

Do not ignore it, and do not assume it is a mistake even if you are confident it is.

Most Insurance Advisory Letters are resolved by establishing one of three things: the vehicle is insured and the database is out of date, the vehicle has been sold and the record is stale, or the vehicle is off the road and needs a SORN. All three are fixable quickly, and all three become considerably more expensive once a penalty has been issued and the matter has moved on from correspondence.

The one response that never works is waiting to see whether it escalates. It does.

- **£300 and six points** at the roadside; **unlimited fine and disqualification** in court
- Six points **revokes a new driver's licence** outright
- The vehicle can be **seized immediately**, with fees to recover it
- **IN10** sits on the record for four years and is among the costliest endorsements for premiums
- **Continuous Insurance Enforcement** penalises uninsured cars that never move — a SORN is the way out
- Detection is **automatic** via ANPR against the Motor Insurance Database
- **Driving Other Cars cover is mostly gone** — check the certificate, do not assume
