A car sitting on a driveway with no tax is in one of two situations. Either the DVLA has been told it is off the road, in which case nothing is wrong, or it has not been told, in which case penalties are accruing against a vehicle that has not moved in months.

The difference is one free declaration that takes a couple of minutes. The gap between the two outcomes is considerable.

## What SORN actually means

A Statutory Off Road Notification tells the DVLA that a vehicle will not be used or kept on a public road.

Once it is recorded:

- The vehicle **does not need to be taxed**
- You are **not required to insure it** — the continuous insurance requirement is lifted
- You are **not required to MOT it** while it stays off the road
- Any **full months of remaining tax are refunded** automatically

In exchange, the vehicle must genuinely be off the public road — a garage, a driveway, private land. Not a residential street, which is a public road for this purpose however quiet it is and however clearly the car belongs to the house it is parked outside.

That last point is where most honest mistakes happen. People reason that the car is outside their own home, not bothering anyone, and therefore off the road in any sense that matters. The legislation does not see it that way. If the kerb is public highway, the car is on a public road.

## When you need one

You need a SORN if the vehicle will be kept off the road and you do not intend to tax it. In practice that means:

- The car is laid up for winter, or part-restored, or waiting for a repair you cannot currently afford
- It has failed its MOT and will sit until it is fixed
- You are between uses and would rather not pay tax for months you will not drive

Worth knowing: **tax is payable for any calendar month in which the car is used on the road at all.** Driving it once on the 2nd of the month means that month is taxable. So the rule of thumb is that if the vehicle will be unused for more than a month, a SORN is usually the right call.

A SORN **does not expire**. It remains in force until the vehicle is taxed again. The annual renewal requirement disappeared years ago, which is a common source of outdated advice.

## What it costs to get wrong

Two separate failures, two separate penalties.

**Failing to declare.** If a vehicle is untaxed and has not been declared off the road, the fixed penalty is **£80**, reduced to **£40** if paid within 33 days. This is the one most people meet.

Beyond the fixed penalty, the DVLA can pursue an **out-of-court settlement** that scales with how long the vehicle has been untaxed, **clamp** it where it stands, or prosecute — and court fines run considerably higher. Enforcement is largely automated: the DVLA runs its records against the tax database continuously, so a vehicle that is neither taxed nor SORN'd is identified without anyone walking past it.

**Driving a SORN'd vehicle.** This is the expensive one: up to **£2,500**.

Note the asymmetry, because it is counterintuitive. Driving a car you never declared off the road is an untaxed-vehicle offence. Driving one you *did* declare off the road is a worse offence, because you have told the DVLA in writing that it would not be on the road and then put it there.

## The single journey that is allowed

There is one exception. You may drive a SORN'd vehicle on a public road **to or from a pre-booked MOT or other statutory test appointment.**

The conditions are the same as for driving an untested car: the appointment must already exist, the journey should be direct, and the vehicle must be roadworthy and insured for that journey. Our guide to [driving without an MOT](/blog/driving-without-an-mot-penalties) covers that overlap in more detail, because a SORN'd car coming back onto the road usually needs a test first.

Everything else — moving it to a different driveway, taking it for a run to charge the battery, driving it to a buyer — requires tax.

## The insurance question

You are not legally obliged to insure a SORN vehicle. Whether you should is a separate decision, and the honest answer is usually yes.

A car in a garage is still exposed to fire, flood, theft and a tree coming through the roof. **Laid-up cover** exists for exactly this: it insures the vehicle against those risks while excluding road use, and costs a fraction of a normal policy.

The calculation is simple enough. If the car is worth having, it is worth covering against the ways it can be destroyed while parked.

## Bringing it back

There is no form to cancel a SORN. **Taxing the vehicle ends it automatically.**

The sequence matters, because each step depends on the one before:

1. **MOT** — you cannot tax a vehicle without a valid certificate, and a car that has been laid up for months usually needs a test
2. **Insurance** — needs to be in place before the car is taxed and used
3. **Tax** — which ends the SORN the moment it takes effect

The MOT is the step that catches people out. A vehicle that has sat for a year will often need work before it passes, and until it passes it cannot be taxed — meaning it cannot legally be driven anywhere except to the test it has already failed once. Budget for that before you plan the car back into service.

## The short version

- SORN is **free**, takes minutes, and refunds the full months of tax left
- It **never expires** — it lasts until the vehicle is taxed again
- A residential street is a **public road**; a driveway is not
- Not declaring: **£80**, halved to £40 within 33 days, plus possible clamping or prosecution
- Driving a SORN'd car: **up to £2,500** — worse than never having declared at all
- The only permitted journey is **to a pre-booked MOT**
- To return it to the road: **MOT, then insurance, then tax** — the SORN ends by itself
