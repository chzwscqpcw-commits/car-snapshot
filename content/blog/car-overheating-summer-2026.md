A temperature gauge creeping towards the red — especially in summer traffic — is one warning you genuinely can't drive through. Overheating can turn a cheap fix into a four-figure engine bill in minutes. Here's why it happens, exactly what to do in the moment, and how to stop it.

## Why cars overheat

Your engine makes a lot of heat, and the cooling system's whole job is to carry it away. When something in that system fails, the heat builds. The usual suspects:

- **Low coolant** — usually from a leak; the most common cause
- **A stuck thermostat** — jammed closed, so coolant can't circulate
- **A failed water pump** — no longer moving coolant through the engine
- **A broken cooling fan** — so the car runs hot in traffic but cools on the move
- **A blocked or clogged radiator** — reducing how much heat it can shed

Summer doesn't cause these on its own — but hot weather and heavy, stop-start traffic pile extra load onto the cooling system, so any hidden weakness shows up exactly when you least want it to.

## What to do the moment it happens

If the gauge climbs into the red or a temperature warning light appears while you're driving, act fast and calmly:

1. **Turn off the air conditioning** — it adds load to the engine.
2. **Turn the heater to full hot and the fan up.** Counter-intuitive, but it pulls heat away from the engine and can buy you time.
3. **Pull over safely** as soon as you can and **switch off the engine.**
4. **Do not open the bonnet straight away**, and never remove the coolant cap while hot — the system is pressurised and can spray scalding steam. **Wait at least 30 minutes** to cool.
5. Once cool, **check the coolant level** and top up if low.

If it overheats again quickly, don't push on — call for help. A short tow is far cheaper than a new engine.

## Why you can't just keep going

This is the part that costs people thousands. Driving on an overheating engine can **warp the cylinder head or blow the head gasket** — repairs that often run well into four figures. The engine doesn't give you much warning between "running hot" and "serious damage," so the gauge in the red is a stop-now signal, not a "make it home" one.

## Checking your coolant

With the engine **cold**, find the coolant expansion tank — a translucent plastic reservoir under the bonnet with **min and max** marks. The level should sit between them. If it's low, top up with the correct coolant/antifreeze mix for your car.

But here's the key: if you keep having to top it up, you have a **leak**, not just a low level. Topping up masks the problem; finding and fixing the leak solves it.

## Preventing it

- **Keep coolant topped up** and at the right concentration year-round.
- **Have the cooling system checked** if your car is older or you've noticed it running warm.
- **Watch the gauge in traffic** — a car that heats up at a standstill but cools when moving often has a failing fan, an easy fix if caught early.

A summer service that includes a coolant and cooling-system check is cheap insurance against a roadside breakdown in 30-degree heat.

## The bottom line

Overheating is one of the few faults where your reaction in the first 60 seconds decides whether it's a £60 fix or a £1,500 one. Stop as soon as it's safe, never open a hot system, and treat repeated top-ups as a leak to fix — not a habit.
