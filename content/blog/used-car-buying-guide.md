Buying a used car is the largest purchase most people make without any professional help. There is no survey, no conveyancer and, if you buy privately, almost no legal protection once the money changes hands. The good news is that nearly every expensive mistake is avoidable, and most of them are avoidable *before you leave the house*.

This guide walks through the whole process in order: working out what you can actually afford, checking a car remotely, reading a listing properly, inspecting and driving the car, verifying the paperwork, and paying safely.

## Start with the real budget, not the sticker price

The advertised price is the smallest decision you will make. What matters is the total cost of the first year, and it varies enormously between two cars sitting at the same price.

- **Road tax (VED).** Rates depend on when the car was first registered and its CO2 emissions. Two similar-looking cars a year apart in age can differ by hundreds of pounds a year. Check the [tax status and rate](/tax-check) before you commit.
- **Insurance.** Get a real quote for the specific car before you buy, not an estimate. Insurance groups vary sharply between trim levels of the same model — a slightly larger engine or a sportier badge can move it several groups.
- **Fuel.** Compare the [real running costs](/running-costs) rather than the manufacturer's official figures, which are optimistic for almost every car on the road.
- **ULEZ and Clean Air Zones.** If you drive into London or another charging zone, a non-compliant car costs £12.50 every single day. [Check compliance](/ulez-check) before you buy — this one catches people out constantly.
- **Imminent work.** If the MOT expires in two months and the last test listed advisories, you are buying those repairs. Price them in.

A car £500 cheaper that needs two tyres, has an advisory on the brakes and sits one ULEZ band the wrong side of the line is not cheaper.

## Check the car before you travel

This is the highest-value ten minutes in the entire process, and it costs nothing. Ask the seller for the registration number. Any legitimate seller will give it — refusal is itself an answer.

Enter it into a [free car check](/car-check) and you will see:

- **[Full MOT history](/mot-check)** — every test, pass or fail, with the advisories and failure reasons going back years
- **[Mileage at every test](/mileage-check)** — the odometer reading logged at each MOT, so you can see whether the trend makes sense
- **[Tax status](/tax-check)** and [ULEZ compliance](/ulez-check)
- **[Outstanding safety recalls](/recall-check)** that have never been actioned
- **The DVLA details** — make, model, engine size, fuel type, colour and date of first registration
- **A [valuation](/car-valuation)** so you know whether the asking price is sensible

Read the MOT history properly rather than glancing at the pass/fail. You are looking for patterns: the same advisory appearing year after year and never being fixed, a long gap between a failure and the retest, corrosion advisories that escalate in wording, or a sudden change of test centre. Any of these tell you how the car has been treated.

Then check the mileage trend. Roughly 7,000–9,000 miles a year is typical for a UK car. A reading that drops between tests is a hard stop. But so is a reading that suddenly *flattens* — a car that covered 12,000 miles a year for six years and then 1,500 in the last two has either been off the road or had its history interfered with. Ask why, and get an answer that matches the paperwork.

Crucially, check that everything matches the advert. If the listing says 1.6 diesel and the DVLA record says 1.4 petrol, you have either a careless seller or a cloned plate. Walk away.

## Red flags in the listing

Before you contact anyone, read the advert like a sceptic:

- **The number plate is blurred, cropped or covered.** Occasionally a privacy preference; more often it prevents you doing the check above. Ask for it directly.
- **The description is vague.** "Runs and drives", "any inspection welcome", "no time wasters" and little else. Genuine sellers who have looked after a car tend to say so in detail.
- **No mention of service history.** A missing service book is not automatically fatal on an older car, but it should be reflected in the price and explained.
- **The price is well below market.** Compare against the [valuation](/car-valuation) for that model, age and mileage. A genuinely cheap car has a reason, and you want to know it before you travel, not after.
- **The photos are too few, too small, or taken at night.** Darkness hides paint defects and panel gaps very effectively.
- **The seller pushes for a fast deposit.** Urgency is the oldest lever in fraud. A real car will still be there tomorrow.
- **The address or location keeps shifting.** Meeting "somewhere convenient" rather than at the seller's home address is a serious warning sign for a private sale — the V5C should show that address.

## Inspecting the car in person

View it in daylight, in dry weather if you can, and at the seller's home address. Rain hides paint defects; darkness hides everything.

**Bodywork.** Crouch at each corner and look down the length of the car. Panel gaps should be even and consistent side to side. Look for colour mismatch between panels, overspray on rubber seals or trim, and paint texture that changes from one panel to the next. Small dents and stone chips are normal wear. A repainted panel means something happened, and you want to know what.

**Tyres.** Check all four, and the spare. Tread must be at least 1.6mm across the central three-quarters, but the useful information is *how* they are worn. Wear on one edge only suggests tracking or suspension problems. Four mismatched budget brands on a premium car tells you how the previous owner economised. Two brand-new tyres on one axle can mean a fresh MOT pass — check the MOT record to see whether tyres were an advisory.

**Under the bonnet.** You do not need to be a mechanic. Look for obvious oil leaks around the engine and on the ground beneath, check the oil on the dipstick is not black sludge or milky (milky suggests a head gasket problem), and check the coolant level and colour. A suspiciously spotless engine bay on an older car can mean a recent steam clean to hide a leak.

**Interior.** Check that wear matches the claimed mileage. A steering wheel worn shiny, a driver's seat bolster collapsed and shiny pedal rubbers on a car showing 40,000 miles do not belong together. Test every electrical item: windows, mirrors, air conditioning, heated seats, infotainment, all the lights.

**Cold start.** Insist the engine is cold when you arrive — ask the seller not to warm it up. A cold start reveals rattles, smoke and starting problems that vanish once the engine is warm. Blue smoke suggests burning oil, white smoke that persists suggests coolant, and black smoke suggests a fuelling problem. A brief puff on a cold diesel is normal; a continuing cloud is not.

## The test drive

Twenty minutes minimum, over varied roads — town, a faster A-road, and a rough surface if you can find one. Turn the radio off and open a window.

- **Brakes.** They should stop the car straight, without pulling to one side, and without vibration through the pedal or wheel. Find a safe empty stretch and brake firmly once.
- **Steering.** Straight and centred on a level road, with no vibration at speed and no knocking on full lock.
- **Gearbox.** Manual: every gear should engage cleanly without grinding or jumping out, and the clutch should bite somewhere sensible rather than at the very top of its travel. Automatic: shifts should be smooth and prompt, with no flaring between gears or heavy thumps.
- **Suspension.** Over a rough surface, listen for knocks and clonks. The car should settle after a bump rather than continuing to bounce.
- **Noises.** A whine that changes pitch with road speed usually points at wheel bearings. A rhythmic knock rising with engine speed is more serious.
- **Warning lights.** Every light should illuminate on ignition and go out once running. A seller who has removed a bulb to hide a fault is not hypothetical — check they all appear first.

Afterwards, let it idle and look underneath for fresh drips, then check the temperature gauge sits where it should.

## The paperwork that actually matters

This is where the expensive problems live, and none of them are visible on a test drive.

**The V5C logbook.** Check the registration, VIN and engine number on the document match the car. The VIN is usually visible at the base of the windscreen and on a door pillar sticker. Confirm the seller's name and address match the V5C and the address you are standing at. A seller who is not the registered keeper needs a very good explanation. Check the document itself for signs of alteration — and be aware that a genuine V5C has a watermark.

**Service history.** Stamps in a book are helpful but easy to forge. Invoices with garage names, dates and mileages are far better evidence, and you can call the garage. Cross-reference the mileages on the invoices with the [MOT mileage record](/mileage-check).

**Outstanding finance, write-off and theft records.** These are the three that cost people thousands, and none of them appear in free public data:

1. **Outstanding finance.** If money is still owed, the finance company legally owns the car — not the seller, and not you. It can be repossessed, and you have little recourse against a private seller who has disappeared. This is the single most common reason to run a paid check.
2. **Write-off history.** A car recorded as a total loss and repaired can be perfectly safe, but it is worth considerably less, and the quality of the repair matters enormously. An undisclosed write-off is both a financial hit and a safety question.
3. **Mileage fraud.** A single MOT-history lookup catches clumsy clocking. It does not catch a car wound back before it was imported, or discrepancies recorded outside the MOT system.

## Consider a professional inspection

For anything expensive, unfamiliar or bought at distance, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified mechanic is the best money in the process. It covers what a history report cannot: the mechanical condition of the actual car. A report covers the paperwork; an inspection covers the metal. Between them there is very little left to surprise you.

## Paying safely

- **Never send a large sum before you have seen the car.** No legitimate private sale requires it.
- **Avoid bank transfers to sellers you have not met.** Once sent, the money is effectively gone.
- **Use a credit card or PayPal for deposits** where the seller accepts them, for the buyer protection.
- **Get a receipt** stating the registration, VIN, price, date, and both names and addresses — and the words "sold as seen" if that is the basis, so both sides know where they stand.
- **Complete the V5C transfer** with the seller at the point of sale. Do not leave it to be posted later.
- **Tax the car before you drive it away.** Road tax no longer transfers with the vehicle, so it must be taxed in your name before it moves.

## Walk-away warning signs

Some things are not worth negotiating over. Walk away if:

- The seller will not provide the registration number
- The V5C is missing, or the seller is not the registered keeper without a clear explanation
- The VIN on the car does not match the V5C
- The DVLA record contradicts the advert on engine, fuel type or colour
- The mileage drops between MOT tests, or the seller cannot explain a sudden change in the trend
- A paid report shows outstanding finance the seller did not disclose
- You are pressured to decide immediately, or to pay before viewing
- The engine was already warm when you arrived, despite asking

There is always another car. The cost of walking away is an afternoon; the cost of ignoring these is measured in thousands.

## Your buying checklist

1. Work out the true first-year cost — tax, insurance, fuel, ULEZ, imminent repairs
2. Get the registration and run a [free car check](/car-check)
3. Read the MOT history for patterns, and the mileage for a sensible trend
4. Confirm the DVLA record matches the advert
5. Read the listing sceptically for red flags
6. View in daylight, at the seller's address, with the engine cold
7. Check bodywork, tyres, under the bonnet, interior wear and every electrical item
8. Test drive for twenty minutes with the radio off
9. Verify the V5C, VIN and service history
10. Run a paid history report for finance, write-off and mileage fraud
11. Get a professional inspection on anything significant
12. Pay safely, complete the V5C, and tax it before you drive away

## The bottom line

Most used car disasters are not bad luck. They are checks that were skipped — usually because the buyer had already fallen for the car, driven a long way, or felt awkward asking. The two habits that prevent nearly all of them are cheap and unglamorous: check the registration before you travel, and check the paperwork before you pay.
