Most drivers know a speeding ticket costs £100 and three points. That is true of the common case and misleading about everything else, because at a certain speed the system stops issuing fixed penalties altogether and starts calculating what you earn.

The jump is not gradual. There is a line, and on the far side of it a £100 ticket becomes a court date and a fine measured as a share of your income.

## Two completely different systems

**The fixed penalty.** For speeds modestly over the limit, the police issue a conditional offer: **£100 and three penalty points**. You accept it, pay it, and it is over. No court, no hearing, no discretion involved.

**The court route.** Above that threshold, there is no fixed penalty option. The case goes before magistrates, who sentence using bands expressed as a percentage of your **weekly income** — which means the same offence costs two drivers wildly different amounts.

This is the part that catches people out. There is no "bigger ticket" for faster driving. There is a ticket, and then there is a court.

## The bands

Magistrates use three bands, set by how far over the limit you were *relative to the limit* — 35 in a 30 is treated differently from 85 in a 70, even though both are five miles over, because the proportion differs.

| Band | Roughly | Fine | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Lowest bracket, modestly over | ~50% of weekly income | 3 points |
| **B** | Middle bracket | ~100% of weekly income | 4–6 points, or 7–28 day ban |
| **C** | Highest bracket | ~150% of weekly income | 6 points, or 7–56 day ban |

Two caps apply on top: **£1,000** maximum for most roads, **£2,500** on a motorway.

Band C is where a short disqualification becomes a realistic outcome for a single offence, with no previous history required. A driver on a clean licence can leave court banned.

Magistrates can move up or down within a band for aggravating or mitigating factors — towing, carrying passengers for hire, poor weather, or proximity to a school all push upward; a genuine emergency pushes down. There is also the standard one-third reduction for an early guilty plea.

## The speed awareness course

For lower-end offences, many forces offer a **speed awareness course** instead of a fine and points. Complete it and you receive neither — no money paid to the court, nothing on your licence.

The conditions are consistent across forces, with some local variation:

- You must **not have attended one in the previous three years**
- The speed must fall within the band the course covers — the lower end of the range
- It is **offered at the discretion of the police**, never demanded

Courses typically cost **£80 to £100** — roughly the price of the fine you are avoiding — and run about four hours, now commonly online.

The three-year rule is the one that bites. It runs from the date you *attended*, not the date of the offence, and it is checked nationally. A driver who took a course in year one and is caught again in year two is going to be offered the fixed penalty instead.

## The paperwork, and the trap inside it

A camera-detected offence produces a **Notice of Intended Prosecution** and a **Section 172 request** — a legal demand to identify who was driving.

The NIP must normally reach the **registered keeper within 14 days** of the offence. Late service is a genuine basis to challenge, with one large caveat that is entirely within your control: the 14-day rule runs to the keeper at the address the DVLA holds. If your V5C still shows a flat you moved out of two years ago, the notice going astray is your problem, not the prosecution's.

The Section 172 request is where people do real damage to themselves. **Failing to identify the driver is a worse offence than speeding** — six penalty points and a fine of up to £1,000. There is a defence where you genuinely could not have known despite reasonable diligence, but it requires showing what steps you actually took.

Returning the form late, ignoring it, or sending back something evasive is reliably more expensive than the three points you were trying to avoid. If the car is used by several people, keeping a note of who had it when is not paranoia — it is the evidence that makes the answer honest.

## Points, and the threshold that ends a licence

Points for most speeding offences stay on a licence for **four years from the offence date**, though they count toward disqualification for three.

**Twelve points within three years** triggers a totting-up disqualification, normally **six months**. Magistrates can decline to disqualify on grounds of "exceptional hardship", but the bar is genuinely high — losing your job is frequently argued and frequently rejected, because the court's position is that this is the ordinary consequence of the penalty rather than an exceptional one.

**New drivers face a much lower threshold.** Within two years of passing, **six points revokes the licence entirely**. Not a suspension — a revocation. You return to a provisional licence and must retake both the theory and practical tests. Two speeding offences do it.

## Before you respond to anything, check the plate

One scenario is worth ruling out before you fill in a Section 172 form naming yourself.

If the notice relates to a road you have never driven, at a time you can account for, you may be looking at a **cloned plate** — someone running your registration on a similar vehicle. It is not common, but it happens, and the first symptom is usually exactly this: penalties for journeys you did not make.

The second possibility is that the vehicle was sold and the DVLA record never updated, so notices are still arriving at your door for someone else's driving.

Either way the answer starts with the record. A [free car check](/car-check) shows what the DVLA currently holds — make, model, colour, engine size and tax status. If the notice says a silver van and the record says your blue hatchback, you have an immediate and complete answer, and something considerably more urgent than a speeding ticket to deal with.

## The short version

- **£100 and three points** is the fixed penalty, and it covers the ordinary case
- Beyond that there is no bigger ticket — there is **court**, and a fine set as a share of **weekly income**
- Bands run **A (50%) / B (100%) / C (150%)**, capped at £1,000, or £2,500 on a motorway
- A **speed awareness course** is offered, not claimed, and not within three years of the last one
- The NIP must reach the keeper within **14 days** — at the address on your V5C
- **Not naming the driver is worse than speeding**: six points and up to £1,000
- Twelve points means a ban; **six ends a new driver's licence** entirely
